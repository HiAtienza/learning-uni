# Context-Free Grammars and Languages

[[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/ADS205/Tutor Mode/00 Start Here|Tutor Start Here]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/ADS205/Tutor Mode/Unit 05 - Context-Free Grammars and Languages/02 Recall and Exam Practice|Recall and Practice]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/ADS205/Unit 05 - Context-Free Grammars and Languages/01 Notes|Ordinary reference]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/ADS205/Unit 05 - Context-Free Grammars and Languages/03 Lab|Lab]]

## In 60 Seconds

A grammar is a finite recipe for generating words. Variables mark unfinished work; terminals make up finished words. Recursion can express matched counts or nesting that a finite automaton cannot track without bound. A derivation lists replacements, whereas a parse tree records their structure. Different grammars may generate the same language, yet disagree on grouping. After this unit, derive a word, justify a CFG's exact language, combine grammars and encode precedence/association rather than assuming that rule order gives them.[^l4]

The connected ideas are ==generation==, ==terminal yield==, ==recursive invariant==, ==structural ambiguity== and ==precedence levels==. You will use Unit 01's set reasoning and Unit 02's DFA semantics; Unit 04 supplies the equal-count/nonregular contrast.

## Concept Preview

> [!note] Concept — A reusable production
> **Plain-language meaning:** A variable can be replaced by a finite RHS wherever it occurs; its neighbours are not a condition.
> **Why it matters:** One rule may be reused to create an unbounded family of finite words.
> **Connects to:** recursion, base cases and CFG tuples.
> **Source:** Lecture 4 PDF pp.5–8.

> [!note] Concept — Generated language
> **Plain-language meaning:** Collect only terminal words obtainable from the designated start, not unfinished forms.
> **Why it matters:** A correct rule set with a different start may generate a different language.
> **Connects to:** membership witnesses and both-direction proofs.
> **Source:** Lecture 4 p.11; Tutorial 4 pp.1–2, P1–P2.

> [!note] Concept — Ordered parse tree
> **Plain-language meaning:** The tree records nested choices; reading leaves left to right gives the word.
> **Why it matters:** Replacement schedules can differ without changing the tree.
> **Connects to:** leftmost/rightmost derivations and ambiguity.
> **Source:** Lecture 4 pp.22–24; Tutorial 4 pp.3–4, P5.

> [!note] Concept — Grammar composition
> **Plain-language meaning:** A new start selects one grammar, joins two, or repeatedly chooses a factor.
> **Why it matters:** It proves CFL closure under union, concatenation and star.
> **Connects to:** disjoint variable names and finite concatenations.
> **Source:** Lecture 4 pp.14–19.

> [!note] Concept — Expression structure
> **Plain-language meaning:** Separate variables give precedence levels; recursion direction gives association.
> **Why it matters:** Equal terminal strings may hide different grouping.
> **Connects to:** grammar versus language ambiguity.
> **Source:** Lecture 4 pp.20–24; Tutorial 4 pp.4–7, P6–P7.

> [!success]- Check your answer

## A motivating question — A generator and a parser

**Synthetic case grounded in T4.** A processor needs (a) exactly a^n b^n including epsilon and (b) a unique intended structure for a+a+a and a↑b↑c. Can a finite grammar do both? Success requires a general language argument and a structural argument; matching one output is not enough.

| Question | What you need |
|---|---|
| Why do counts match? | recursive invariant and base rule |
| Why does order match? | insertion around the remaining variable |
| Do two schedules mean ambiguity? | tree versus chronology |
| Which operator groups first? | precedence and association |

## Let the unfinished middle carry the dependency

Start with $S\to aSb\mid\varepsilon$. Intuitively, every unfinished middle receives one new a on its left and b on its right. Formally, after n recursive steps the form is $a^nSb^n$; that is the invariant. Choosing epsilon removes the only variable and leaves $a^nb^n$.

For aaabbb, keep the unfinished S visible:
$S\Rightarrow aSb\Rightarrow aaSbb\Rightarrow aaaSbbb\Rightarrow aaabbb$.
Before the last step we have a **sentential form**, not a word in the language, because S is not a terminal. “⇒*” allows zero or more replacement steps, whereas “→” declares a rule. These arrows answer different questions.[^t4]

Why is the language exact? No derivation can insert b to the left of a, and every recursive application adds the same count. Conversely, any desired n can be achieved by n recursive choices and one terminating choice. Both inclusions matter. Therefore aabbb and abab fail for different reasons: unequal counts versus incorrect order.

This grammar is unambiguous: the target length fixes the number of recursive layers and each layer has only one S to expand. That does not mean all recursive grammars are unambiguous.

> [!question] Explain it yourself
> Why does S→0S11 generate two ones per zero without storing a numerical counter?

> [!success]- Check your explanation
> Each recursive layer adds one 0 before the remaining variable and two 1s after it. After n layers the invariant is $0^nS1^{2n}$; termination removes S.
> Source: Tutorial 4 PDF p.3, P4.

## See a tree rather than just a sequence

For S→00S1|ε, the word 000011 requires two recursive layers. The ordered children at each layer are 0,0,S,1, not merely a set of symbols.

![[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/ADS205/Unit 05 - Context-Free Grammars and Languages/Attachments/parse-tree.svg]]

**Figure 1 — Structure fixes the terminal yield**

**Read it:** descend through the middle S nodes, then read all terminal leaves from left to right, omitting epsilon.
**Key takeaway:** the yield is 000011; the leftmost and rightmost derivations coincide because one variable remains at each step.
**Provenance:** technical redraw of Tutorial 4 P5, pp.3–4; tutor-local copy avoids ordinary-output aliasing.

Contrast the pedagogical grammar S→AB, A→a, B→b. S⇒AB⇒aB⇒ab and S⇒AB⇒Ab⇒ab apply choices in different orders, but both trees have S above ordered children A and B. This is not ambiguity. To prove ambiguity, exhibit **two structures** or **two distinct leftmost derivations**, not a leftmost and a rightmost schedule for one structure.

## Compose whole languages by composing starts

Let renamed CFGs generate A and B. Introduce fresh X. X→S_A|S_B selects one whole derivation for union. X→S_A S_B puts two independent derivations next to each other for concatenation. X→S_A X|ε chooses a finite sequence of A-words for star. Old rules remain available; renaming variables prevents cross-expansion.[^l4closure]

The star construction handles zero factors explicitly through X→ε. It also handles epsilon in A: extra empty factors may yield another description, not another distinct terminal word. Construction correctness is about the generated set, not necessarily uniqueness.

Every DFA can also become a CFG: assign A_q to each state, add A_q→aA_r for each q— a→r transition and A_f→ε for accepting f. The variable records the state reached after the generated prefix. Epsilon termination is possible exactly at an accepting state. Thus **regular ⇒ context-free**, not conversely. Equal-count words supply the counterexample to the converse.[^l4regular]

## Why grouping cannot be guessed from the printed word

For S→S+S|a, a+a+a has trees (a+a)+a and a+(a+a). One expands the left subtree at the top split; the other the right. Both can be written as different leftmost derivations, so this is genuine grammar ambiguity. A left-recursive replacement S→S+a|a has the same terminal language $a(+a)^n$ but fixes left grouping.[^t4ambiguity]

**A second problem before its solution:** the reference E/T/F grammar handles + and *, but how can ↑ bind tighter and group rightward? Adding ↑ at E's level would not enforce stronger precedence. Insert P between T and F:
E→E+T|T; T→T*P|P; P→F↑P|F; F→(E)|a|b|c.

Reading from the top: E combines complete products, T complete powers, P complete factors. Deeper structure is grouped inside an outer operand, so a*b↑c means a*(b↑c). The expandable P is to the right of ↑, so a↑b↑c means a↑(b↑c). This says nothing about the direction in which a parser must expand variables during derivation. The reference F lacks c; the supplied P7 solution explicitly adds it to support its example words.

## Return to the motivating question

**Generator:** S→aSb|ε generates all and only $a^nb^n$ by the invariant/converse argument. Epsilon is included through the base; terminal order is maintained through wrapping.

**Parser:** S→S+a|a removes the two possible + groupings while preserving the word set. For a↑b↑c, the P-level right recursion determines a↑(b↑c). The same production hierarchy gives multiplication higher precedence than addition. Neither a unique tree for one tested word nor one successful tool run proves unambiguity for every unrelated grammar.

## 5-Minute Review

A CFG is (V,Σ,R,S), with one variable per rule's left side. Define an invariant for recursive generation and prove both inclusions. Parse trees preserve RHS order; derivation schedules do not by themselves establish ambiguity. Translate DFA states into variables, with epsilon only at accepting states. Rename variables before combining starts. To prove ambiguity, find one word with two trees; to encode expressions, separate precedence levels and place recursion on the association side.

## Atomic recall

### Card 01 — What makes a production context-free?

> [!success]- Answer
> Its left side is one variable; replacement does not require a particular surrounding context.
>
> Source: Lecture 4 pp.7–8.

### Card 02 — What are the four components of a CFG?

> [!success]- Answer
> Finite variables V, finite terminals Σ, finite rules R and a designated start variable S∈V.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 p.1.

### Card 03 — What does the production arrow declare?

> [!success]- Answer
> An allowed replacement rule, rather than an actual derivation step.
>
> Source: Lecture 4 p.11.

### Card 04 — What does the starred derivation relation permit?

> [!success]- Answer
> Zero or more derivation steps.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 p.1.

### Card 05 — When is a derived form a language member?

> [!success]- Answer
> When it contains only terminals and is derived from the designated start variable.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 pp.1–2, P1.4.

### Card 06 — What form appears after n recursive uses of S→aSb?

> [!success]- Answer
> The sentential form aⁿSbⁿ.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 p.2, P2.

### Card 07 — What is read from the leaves of an ordered parse tree?

> [!success]- Answer
> The terminal word in left-to-right order, ignoring epsilon leaves.
>
> Source: Lecture 4 p.23; Tutorial 4 P5.

### Card 08 — What must a leftmost derivation expand next?

> [!success]- Answer
> The leftmost remaining variable.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 p.1.

### Card 09 — What demonstrates grammar ambiguity?

> [!success]- Answer
> One terminal word with two distinct parse trees, equivalently two distinct leftmost derivations.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 pp.1,4, P6.

### Card 10 — Why rename variables when combining CFGs?

> [!success]- Answer
> To prevent rules of one grammar from expanding variables of the other.
>
> Source: Lecture 4 pp.14–19; explicit construction condition.

### Card 11 — Where do epsilon termination rules occur in a DFA-derived grammar?

> [!success]- Answer
> At variables corresponding to accepting DFA states.
>
> Source: Lecture 4 p.13.

### Card 12 — Which side recursively expands for right-associative exponentiation?

> [!success]- Answer
> The right operand: P→F↑P.
>
> Source: Tutorial 4 p.6, P7.

## Guided then independent practice

### Problem — A shortened derivation

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, T4 P2 principles. Under S→aSb|ε, write a derivation of aabb and explain what forbids aab.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Count recursive layers before terminating, then compare the two terminal counts.

> [!success]- Solution
> $S\Rightarrow aSb\Rightarrow aaSbb\Rightarrow aabb$. Every recursive step adds one a and one b; termination adds neither. Therefore all completed words have equal counts, excluding aab. The invariant is $a^nSb^n$, not a list of tested words.

### Problem — Two schedules

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, T4 P1.3/P5. For S→AB, A→0, B→1, exhibit different leftmost/rightmost derivations and decide whether they show ambiguity.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Draw the root's ordered children before comparing replacement times.

> [!success]- Solution
> Leftmost: S⇒AB⇒0B⇒01. Rightmost: S⇒AB⇒A1⇒01. Both use the one tree with S children A,B and leaves 0,1. They do not show ambiguity: replacement order differs, but no production choice or subtree structure differs. There is exactly one production for each variable.

### Problem — Change the generated ratio

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, transfer from T4 P4/P5. Generate $\{0^{3n}1^n:n\ge0\}$ with a CFG. Justify both directions, including the empty word.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Add the required numbers of terminals around a single remaining variable.

> [!success]- Solution
> Use $S\to000S1\mid\varepsilon$. After n recursive steps, $0^{3n}S1^n$ terminates to the required word. No other terminal shape can arise, proving one inclusion; choosing n layers for each n proves the converse. For n=0, S→ε supplies the empty word. The reasoning transfers the invariant rather than testing a longer list.

### Problem — A missing base case

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, L4 star construction. A proposed grammar for A* has new start X and only X→S_A X, with all old A-rules. Explain the failure and repair it.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Can the last X disappear in any finite derivation?

> [!success]- Solution
> Every derivation retains an X because the only X-rule creates another X. No finite terminal word, including epsilon, is generated. Add X→ε. Then choose any finite number k of A-factors using X→S_A X k times, terminate X, and complete each factor. This handles zero factors and establishes exactly A*. Rename variables first to avoid accidental cross-rules.

### Problem — Expose the grouping

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, T4 P6. Under S→S+a|a, determine the structure of a+a+a+a. Is the terminal language changed from S→S+S|a?

> [!tip]- Hint
> All recursive + choices occur inside the left S subtree.

> [!success]- Solution
> The structure is ((a+a)+a)+a. Three uses of S→S+a followed by S→a fix the recursive left branch. Both grammars generate exactly $a(+a)^n$; a grammar's terminal language can stay the same while its possible trees shrink. This particular grammar has one tree for each word because its length determines the number of recursive steps.

### Problem — Competing requirements

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE**, T4 P7. A language designer wants * tighter than +, but ↑ left-associative rather than the source's right-associative ↑. Change only the power level and explain a↑b↑c.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Keep the precedence level, but move its expandable variable to the opposite side.

> [!success]- Solution
> Replace P→F↑P|F by P→P↑F|F. Keep E→E+T|T, T→T*P|P and the same F rules. P still lies inside a product operand, preserving higher precedence than *. Its recursive left child now creates (a↑b)↑c. This is an explicitly labelled variant of the original requirement, not a silent change to T4 P7.
