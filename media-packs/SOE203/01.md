# Introduction and Professional Responsibility

[[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Tutor Mode/00 Start Here|Tutor start]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Tutor Mode/Unit 01 - Introduction and Professional Responsibility/01 Tutor Notes|Tutor notes]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Tutor Mode/Unit 01 - Introduction and Professional Responsibility/02 Recall and Exam Practice|Recall and practice]] · [[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Unit 01 - Introduction and Professional Responsibility/01 Notes|Standard reference]]

## In 60 Seconds

Software engineering begins when other people must depend on software over time. A working program is one part of that commitment. You need to identify the whole product, establish who controls its requirements, judge four different quality attributes, manage change, and communicate honestly about evidence and risk. These ideas connect: ownership determines whose needs matter; quality makes those needs discussable; lifecycle work produces evidence; professional responsibility governs the claims you make.

After studying, you should be able to explain why a demonstration is insufficient, distinguish generic from customized products, diagnose a quality weakness, and justify a release decision within practical constraints. No programming background is required. Source: S15 PDF pp.4–8,17–28,35–41 (full filenames in the cards and references below).

## Before You Start

A **requirement** says what is needed from a system; a **constraint** limits acceptable solutions, such as a budget. A **stakeholder** is someone with a relevant interest in the system. Here these are working meanings needed to understand the lecture's requirements/customer discussion; Unit 04 develops the requirements methods. A **program** expresses instructions, while **configuration** supplies environment-specific information needed to operate it. [^product]

> [!question] Quick prerequisite check
> Does a demonstration establish that somebody else can install and maintain the program?

> [!success]- Answer
> No. It shows behavior in the demonstrated environment, while installation, configuration and maintainable knowledge require their own evidence. Source: S15 PDF pp.6,14–15.

## The motivating question — A release under pressure

**Synthetic case.** A university commissions a room-display service. It runs quickly on its author's laptop. Only that author knows the configuration. Staff cannot correct a room without asking the author. No one checked unauthorized changes. Management says the demonstration looks good and the budget is nearly spent. A room-allocation policy may change after release.

**Your task:** Explain what is missing, who decides scope, what the demonstration proves, how to handle future change, and what to say about release. A successful answer covers the complete product and all four qualities, identifies missing evidence, and proposes a feasible next decision without inventing a numerical target or promising zero defects.

| Question | Knowledge needed |
|---|---|
| What is being delivered? | Program versus professional system |
| Who decides a scope change? | Specification ownership |
| What evidence is missing? | Four quality attributes |
| Why can tomorrow's change break today's behavior? | Dependencies and deterioration |
| What may the team honestly promise? | Constraints and responsibility |

## 1. Why does a working program still need engineering?

**Intuition.** A recipe that only its author can follow is not yet a dependable service for a kitchen. Similarly, a program that runs for its author is not the whole product another person needs. The analogy concerns supporting knowledge, not a claim that software follows physical wear mechanisms.

**Precise idea.** The source describes a software system as software components plus configuration, system documentation and user documentation. Software engineering concerns **all aspects of software production**, including supporting management, within organizational and financial constraints. Computer science emphasizes theory and fundamentals; software engineering emphasizes practical development and delivery. Neither distinction makes theory irrelevant to engineering. [^product]

**Work through the release claim.** First identify the users' task: staff must display and correct rooms. Then identify what has been delivered: one successful execution. Next identify what someone else needs: usable configuration, instructions and system knowledge, plus evidence about intended behavior. The gap between those lists explains why documentation, estimation and relevant discussion are engineering work, even when they add no executable feature.

The lecture's Java screenshot illustrates a program; it does not establish a required language/version. Its `System.out.print("Hello World!")` call is explained in the standard reference. No code is executed in this companion. [^product]

**Worked decision.** Reserve effort for documented configuration and a user check before calling the service ready. The justification is not “documentation is always good”; it is that the next user cannot reproduce the demonstrated behavior without the author's private knowledge. If the budget cannot cover the agreed delivery, negotiate scope or schedule instead of redefining an incomplete demonstration as completion.

> [!question] Explain it yourself
> Why can estimating work and writing user instructions count as engineering, although neither changes the program’s output?

> [!success]- Check your explanation
> Engineering covers all aspects of production under constraints. Estimation supports an achievable commitment; instructions help intended users operate the delivered system. Source: S15 PDF pp.7–9.

## 2. Who owns the specification?

**Problem first.** The university pays a supplier, but can it insist on any future feature? Payment alone does not answer the question.

For a **generic product**, the developer controls the specification for a wider market. For a **customized product**, a particular customer commissions the system and controls its specification through agreement with the developer. This is a distinction about product boundary and authority, not free versus paid software. [^ownership]

**Visible worked comparison.** Suppose the university buys a general image editor. Its users can request an interface change, but an individual customer normally cannot dictate the supplier's roadmap. Now suppose the university commissions its own room service. Its agreed requirements determine the custom product, and later changes must be negotiated with the supplier because they may affect effort and schedule. A generic component can sit inside that customized service; classify the level you are discussing.

Return to the case: “commissions” makes customized development the supported interpretation. The university decides desired scope through agreement; the developer must explain implementation consequences. Neither party can make an added feature consume no resources by calling it a small change.

## 3. How do the four quality attributes guide action?

**Problem first.** The screen responds quickly. Is the product therefore good? The source's four attributes make this question precise. [^quality]

| Attribute | Ask this question | Case evidence and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Maintainability | Can it evolve as needs change? | Knowledge held by one author makes future changes difficult |
| Dependability and security | Can users trust behavior and protection against damage or misuse? | Unauthorized changes were not checked; do not claim protection passed |
| Efficiency | Does it use time, memory and processing resources appropriately? | A quick demonstration is limited evidence for that demonstrated workload |
| Acceptability | Can intended users understand and use it in their environment? | Staff need the author's help for their normal task |

**How to reason step by step:** name the observed fact; connect it to an attribute's meaning; state what the fact does and does not prove; choose evidence addressing the gap. A fast but wrong room display can be responsive and still undependable. An accurate display that staff cannot operate can be unacceptable. These are pedagogical counterexamples to the inference “one strength means total quality.”

**Worked plan.** Have a second person reproduce the setup and explain a change, check permitted and unauthorized operations, agree a representative workload before measuring performance, and ask intended staff to complete their task. These are proposed evidence-gathering actions, not reported successful tests. The source supplies no performance target for this invented service, so do not silently invent one.

The lecturer groups **dependability and security together** as one of four attributes. Lab Q1 asks for four additional attributes but lists five suggestions; any appropriate four should be explained. Usability, for example, overlaps with acceptability rather than becoming a fifth essential row. [^quality]

## 4. Why does software deteriorate if it does not wear out?

**Intuition.** Changing a shared assumption can disturb code that still looks untouched. Logical relationships matter even when there is no physical material to erode.

The source's curves show an idealized fall in failure rate as faults are fixed, then a schematic “actual” curve with spikes after changes. They illustrate possible new errors and interacting changes, not measured probabilities or a law that every change must fail. [^change]

![[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Unit 01 - Introduction and Professional Responsibility/Attachments/change-and-failure.svg]]

**Figure 1 — Following change into possible failure risk**

**Read it:** Follow the changed need through modification and possible side effects, then the controls used to reduce them. An arrow is a possible causal link, not a guaranteed failure.

**Key takeaway:** Control dependencies and evidence around change; waiting alone does not wear out code.

**Provenance:** Reused unchanged teaching SVG from the standard edition, based on S15 PDF pp.21–23; informal causal diagram, not UML.

**Worked change.** A new allocation rule alters which room is displayed. First clarify the actual rule with stakeholders to avoid implementing the wrong change. Next identify the responsible part of the software and affected interactions. Make the change in a maintainable structure; then recheck the new behavior and behavior that used to work. Keep supporting information current so later engineers can understand the result. This operationalizes the source's requirements work, modularity, maintainability and comprehensive testing. [^change]

**Limit.** Refusing all changes may preserve old behavior while making the product useless. Testing reduces uncertainty; it cannot establish the absence of every possible fault. Likewise, maintaining an existing system often preserves valuable knowledge, but the lab's strong preference for maintenance does not prove replacement is never justified. [^challenges]

## 5. How do lifecycle and responsibility change the release decision?

**Problem first.** Management counts visible code as progress and asks the team to say “ready.” What work and obligations does that claim hide?

The four fundamental activities are specification, design and implementation, validation, and evolution. They establish needs, construct a solution, check it against needs, and change it over time. They are activities, not a rule that each happens only once. Estimation, coordination and documentation support them. [^process]

The long delivery time and high cost discussed in Lab Q2 follow from skilled work across these activities and the rework caused by dependencies. Rare inputs and environments help explain why not every fault is found before delivery. Embedded institutional knowledge helps explain maintenance effort. Intangibility and incomplete integration make “90% done” harder to verify than a count of physical objects. Each explanation connects a mechanism to a consequence instead of merely calling software complex. [^challenges]

Application context changes the balance: stand-alone applications, interactive transaction systems, embedded control, batch processing, entertainment, simulation and data collection do not have identical constraints. The lecture's aircraft versus e-commerce contrast emphasizes criticality versus evolving requirements. Its web discussion emphasizes reuse, browser constraints and learning through increments for diverse users. AJAX and cloud-pricing remarks are historical source examples, not required technologies or current market claims. [^process]

**Professional responsibility** extends to the effects of requirements, plans, tests and manuals as well as programs. The source names confidentiality, competence, intellectual property rights and computer misuse. In the case, competence includes representing what was checked honestly; confidentiality means entrusted information is not a convenient public debugging artifact. Client satisfaction does not cancel these obligations. [^ethics]

**Visible worked argument.** “The client likes it, therefore release is responsible” has an unsupported step: satisfaction does not show that unauthorized modification was checked. A defensible response reports the successful demonstration and its limits, identifies the unfinished checks, and negotiates a release decision that respects scope, budget and affected users. Certification can support evidence of a minimum professional standard, but it cannot substitute for this product-specific reasoning. [^ethics]

## Return to the motivating question

1. **Deliver the whole system:** make configuration, instructions and maintainable knowledge part of the deliverable, because other people must operate it.
2. **Agree scope with the commissioning university:** treat the service as customized; discuss the costs of later policy changes.
3. **Separate four quality conclusions:** maintainability and acceptability show concrete weaknesses; efficiency has narrow demonstration evidence; dependability/security still lacks the stated check.
4. **Prepare for evolution:** clarify the room rule, contain side effects through maintainable responsibilities, and test changes and affected existing behavior.
5. **Communicate the release boundary:** report evidence and unknowns, then negotiate scope or schedule within budget. Do not claim unperformed validation passed.

This closes every case question without prescribing an unsupported architecture, performance threshold or zero-defect guarantee.

## Guided, independent and transfer practice

### Problem G1 — Interpret a delivery claim

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Week1-Lecture-Introduction.pdf`, PDF pp.5–8.

A developer supplies executable files but no configuration instructions. Explain one specific gap between this delivery and a usable software system.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Compare what the developer knows with what the recipient needs.

> [!success]- Solution
> **Observation:** The executable may depend on settings known only to its author. **Reasoning:** The wider system includes configuration and documentation, so another person needs enough information to reproduce the intended setup. **Action:** Supply documented settings and have the intended recipient attempt setup. This does not prove all other qualities passed.

### Problem I1 — Separate evidence from inference

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Week1-Lecture-Introduction.pdf`, PDF p.17.

A demonstration responds quickly. Staff can use it, but no one has checked its behavior under unauthorized edits. Classify the available evidence and the unknown without assuming any actual security failure.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Differentiate absence of evidence from evidence of a defect.

> [!success]- Solution
> The demonstration supplies limited **efficiency** evidence for its workload and some **acceptability** evidence for the staff task. **Dependability/security** is not established by those facts; lack of the unauthorized-edit check is an evidence gap, not proof that an attack succeeds. Gather the missing evidence before making a protection claim. Maintainability also cannot be inferred from this description.

### Problem D1 — Diagnose the proposed remedy

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Week1-Lecture-Introduction.pdf`, PDF pp.21–23,26.

An engineer proposes preventing all post-release changes to eliminate software deterioration. Evaluate that remedy and propose a better approach.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Consider both correctness of existing behavior and continued usefulness when needs change.

> [!success]- Solution
> Freezing behavior avoids modification-induced faults but may make the product fail new needs. Software evolution remains necessary. Clarify requirements to reduce avoidable rework; organize maintainable modules to limit side effects; test new and affected old behavior. These controls reduce risk rather than guaranteeing zero faults.

### Problem O1 — L5 — Should engineers be certified

**ORIGINAL COURSE EXERCISE** · **Difficulty:** ★★☆

**Source:** Week 1-Lab-Introduction-ExampleAnswers.pdf, PDF p. 3, Q5.

Discuss whether professional software engineers should be certified in the same way as doctors or lawyers.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Separate the public-interest argument, evidence of competence, and the limits of a credential.

> [!success]- Solution
> **Step 1 — Source position.** The suggested answer favors certification because software failures can cause economic damage or endanger people, and a recognized minimum standard supports competence, accountability and trust. Employers also gain a benchmark for assessing candidates.
> 
> **Step 2 — Evaluate the inference.** Demonstrating a minimum standard at certification does not establish that every later decision or product is correct. Continuing competence and product-specific checks still matter.
> 
> **Step 3 — Reasoned discussion.** One defensible position supports certification where the consequences of failure justify it, while requiring an appropriate scope and current evidence. A critical answer can discuss the limits of one credential across varied software work. The source supplies an argument, not a uniquely provable answer or a statement that a particular licensing law applies.

### Problem T1 — A product inside a product

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Week 1-Lab-Introduction-ExampleAnswers.pdf`, PDF p.2 Q3.

A university commissions an equipment-loan application that uses a general-market calendar component. The calendar supplier changes its interface. Explain who controls each specification and how this affects the university team’s promise to preserve the complete workflow.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Choose two product boundaries; ownership at one level does not automatically transfer to another.

> [!success]- Solution
> **Boundary 1:** The calendar is generic; its developer controls its specification, so the university cannot assume authority to prevent supplier changes. **Boundary 2:** The commissioned equipment-loan application is customized; the university controls its agreed requirements through negotiation. **Consequence:** The team must evaluate the supplier change against the application workflow, explain adaptation and validation effort, and negotiate any affected commitment. Using a generic component does not erase the custom system’s requirements. This transfers the ownership distinction to nested product boundaries; it does not establish a particular component contract.
