Requirements Engineering (RE) turns evidence about people's needs into commitments that developers can implement and reviewers can check. This unit connects six ideas: elicitation, analysis, precise specification, validation, traceability and controlled change. The SRS holds the agreed description; its baseline identifies the authorized version.

After studying, you should be able to preserve a stakeholder's intent, separate behavior from quality, choose an EARS pattern, expose an unresolved policy, derive a test, and follow a change through an RTM. The running room-swap case shows why a sentence can sound reasonable while leaving the system's most important decision undefined.

## Concept Preview

> [!info] Evidence before commitment
> **Concept:** Evidence before commitment.
>
> **Plain-language meaning:** Record what was said or observed before deciding how the system should respond. Keep interpretation linked to its source.
>
> **Why it matters:** A plausible solution can otherwise erase the original need.
>
> **Connects to:** elicitation, viewpoints and user requirements.
>
> **Source:** `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material (2).docx`, §§4.4.1–4.4.5, 4.5.2.

> [!info] Conditions and observable responses
> **Concept:** Conditions and observable responses.
>
> **Plain-language meaning:** A precise requirement tells you when an obligation applies and what result to expect. Quality targets also need a measurement rule.
>
> **Why it matters:** This makes missing decisions visible before implementation.
>
> **Connects to:** EARS, FR/NFR and test design.
>
> **Source:** `Requirements Engineering - Lecture Slides ver 1.1.pdf`, PDF pp.32–49,55–57.

> [!info] Requirements interact
> **Concept:** Requirements interact.
>
> **Plain-language meaning:** Two individually reasonable rules can disagree when they govern the same situation.
>
> **Why it matters:** A queue, an urgent request and an approval policy need a joint interpretation.
>
> **Connects to:** interaction grids and validation.
>
> **Source:** `Step-by-step SRS Example.pdf`, PDF pp.14–15.

> [!info] SRS and baseline
> **Concept:** SRS and baseline.
>
> **Plain-language meaning:** The SRS organizes the specification. A baseline is its approved version, maintained through recorded decisions.
>
> **Why it matters:** A filename or blank signature block cannot establish approval.
>
> **Connects to:** governance and change control.
>
> **Source:** `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material (2).docx`, §§4.6.1–4.6.2, 4.8.2.

> [!info] Traceability
> **Concept:** Traceability.
>
> **Plain-language meaning:** Follow a need into its detailed rules, design and tests; follow an artifact back to its justification.
>
> **Why it matters:** A change can affect more than the sentence being edited.
>
> **Connects to:** RTM, impact analysis and evidence status.
>
> **Source:** `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material (2).docx`, §§4.8.1–4.8.2.

## A motivating question

**Official case:** Dr. Li wants urgent room swaps (E-002); Ms. Lee requires her sign-off (E-003). The initial use case creates a pending request and queues a notification. The supplement adds a FIFO waitlist, approved urgent precedence, a 48-hour rule for recurring class-time changes, study-room bookings and maintenance exclusions. [^case]

**Reasoning task:** What must be preserved so “fast swapping” does not bypass approval? Can a capacity check alone satisfy the whole request? What happens if a later change introduces encryption?

| Case question | Knowledge needed |
|---|---|
| Whose rule are we implementing? | Evidence, viewpoints and user/system levels |
| What happens before approval? | EARS, use-case conditions and observable state |
| Can both policies hold? | Interaction analysis and validation |
| What changes after sign-off? | Traceability and ECR/CCB governance |

Success means an explicit, testable interpretation plus visible unresolved decisions. It does not mean inventing a deadline, claiming real stakeholder approval or treating fictional Passed labels as our results.

## 1. How do we avoid specifying the wrong solution?

**Intuition.** Preserve the problem long enough for the people affected to recognize it. A detailed API for an unnecessary operation is still the wrong system.

The course teaches five phases: **0 Feasibility → 1 Elicitation → 2 Analysis & Classification → 3 Specification → 4 Validation**. Feasibility asks whether the proposal is viable; proceed, scope down or cancel are distinct outcomes. Elicitation captures raw needs; analysis organizes and evaluates them; specification assembles precise commitments; validation checks them and sends unresolved findings back. Authorized sign-off establishes a baseline. Management continues as requirements change. [^process]

The internal spiral deepens **Business → User → System** understanding, revisiting elicitation, specification and validation. Analysis is part of the accompanying five-phase account, although the spiral image names three sectors. This iteration can occur within an upfront RE phase. A late misunderstanding may require revising design, code, tests and procedures because those artifacts inherited it; the source does not establish a universal numerical cost multiplier.

### Choose evidence that can answer the missing question

| Missing knowledge | Technique and durable output |
|---|---|
| One person's goals or exceptions | Interview; attributed dated notes and open questions |
| Several conflicting perspectives | Workshop/JAD; speaker-attributed minutes and actions |
| Unspoken habitual work | Observation/shadowing; field notes and current-workflow sketch |
| Input from a wider population | Survey; responses organized by question and sample |
| Existing formal rule | Document analysis; exact policy reference and extracted statement |
| Reaction to a possible interaction | Low-fidelity prototype; annotated screens and feedback |

The **Elicitation Register** holds raw ID, wording, source/technique, date and status. Supporting outputs include draft use-case diagrams/descriptions, stakeholder/viewpoint list, glossary and the records above. Draft material is useful evidence but not yet a finalized authorized requirement. Domain jargon calls for a glossary; tacit work calls for observation; competing departmental interests call for facilitated discussion. [^elicit]

**Worked chain:** E-002 records an urgent-swap problem. Organize it as **UR-2**, an accessible statement of the lecturer's need. Attach E-003's approval constraint; do not discard it because it lacks a separate UR. Refine detailed system rules such as **SR-2.1** capacity rejection. That one rule covers only one part of the larger user need.

A **User Requirement (UR)** communicates stakeholder intent; a **System Requirement (SR)** specifies detailed behavior or constraints for development and testing. Viewpoint-Oriented Analysis asks a different question: Interactor (direct user), Indirect (affected consumer of outputs), or Domain (environment/policy/technical constraint). Abstraction is detail; viewpoint is perspective. A policy communicated by a person can still be a domain constraint. [^analysis]

## 3. How do we turn “quickly, with approval” into a checkable rule?

**Intuition.** Separate the event, the continuing state, the exception and the promised response. Then ask which words still admit competing interpretations.

**EARS** means Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax. Its five basic course patterns structure obligations: [^ears]

| Pattern | Form and appropriate condition |
|---|---|
| Ubiquitous | The system shall respond; no trigger or condition |
| Event-driven | WHEN an event occurs, the system shall respond |
| State-driven | WHILE a state holds, the system shall respond |
| Option-driven | WHERE a feature/configuration is included, the system shall respond |
| Unwanted behavior | IF an unwanted condition holds, THEN the system shall respond |

**Worked decomposition, proposed for the main case:**

1. Preserve E-003 as the reason; preserve Ms. Lee's role.
2. Use an event-driven rule for submission creating a pending request and alerting the administrator.
3. Use an unwanted-behavior rule to prevent allocation change if authorized approval is absent.
4. Define what eligible approval permits and which request it applies to; do not assume approval bypasses capacity, equipment or availability checks.
5. Separate “quickly” into a performance target with agreed timing endpoints, threshold and conditions. Until those are supplied, label the target **UNCONFIRMED**.

An EARS sentence such as “WHEN submitted, the system shall respond quickly” remains ambiguous. Grammar helps expose the missing decision; it cannot supply policy or a number. `WHERE` in the course templates denotes an option/configuration, whereas S11 p.15 uses it for runtime urgent precedence; preserve that source variant while explaining its intended condition.

### Behavior, quality and category are separate questions

An **FR** specifies behavior, input, output or action. An **NFR** specifies quality or constraints on behavior. FURPS+ is the classification checklist: Functionality, Usability, Reliability, Performance, Supportability, plus design/implementation/interface/physical constraints. Category names are not themselves testable commitments. [^quality]

**Worked sentence:** “reset a password securely within 10 seconds” contains password-reset behavior, a performance limit and an undefined security promise. Keep the original 10 seconds; clarify start/end, workload and whether human/email delays count. Elicit the actual security controls. Do not invent them or treat “securely” as an acceptance procedure.

For a measurable quality target, supply **metric + threshold with unit + conditions**. The case's “fetch and render within 3.0 seconds over 4G” provides more than “quickly,” but still needs an operational measurement procedure: start/end events, data volume, workload and repeat policy. A usability proposal can measure completion of a specified task by representative first-time users without help; any proposed success proportion remains non-official. Some constraints are categorical: the goal is objective evaluation, not merely attaching a number.

## 4. What if reasonable requirements compete?

**Problem first.** A routine request arrived first, then an urgent request targets the same room. FIFO and urgent precedence cannot both determine first position without a qualification.

An interaction grid compares pairs: **+1 compatible, 0 neutral, −1 potential conflict** in this classroom scheme. It is symmetric; leave the diagonal unscored. A negative score identifies a question requiring evidence and a decision, not a probability or proof of impossibility. [^conflict]

**Worked policy resolution:** put UR-2 and UR-4 before the relevant stakeholders; walk through this same-room case; log the inconsistent ordering; revise the rule to allow urgent precedence **subject to Ms. Lee's sign-off**, as S11 p.15 does. Recheck cancellation, ties, urgency authority and permissions. The supplement resolves one ambiguity, not all possible queue behavior.

The source also flags encryption versus a short response-time budget. Identify the data path and mandatory controls, evaluate representative overhead and compare feasible designs. If both commitments cannot be met, present scope/resource/target alternatives to the authorized decision-makers. Do not quietly weaken security. In this story encryption starts as an anticipated risk and becomes NFR2 only through the later ECR.

## 5. What makes the SRS ready for a baseline?

**Intuition.** A governed specification needs a readable organization and evidence that its commitments make sense together. Merely collecting every sentence into a file achieves neither.

The course's five top sections are **1 Introduction; 2 Overall Description; 3 Specific Requirements; 4 Appendices; 5 Approval/Sign-off**. Under 3, place FRs by feature/module (3.1), external interfaces (3.2), NFRs by quality/constraint category (3.3), and finalized use cases/models (3.4). The RTM, dictionary, interaction grid and revision history belong in appendices. Stable IDs and cross-references connect the groups. [^srs]

The SRS is the specification; its **baseline** is an authorized version. The Joint Steering Committee considers technical feasibility/completeness, client intent and project budget/schedule. The case uses a contractual scenario; an SRS filename does not universally establish legal enforceability.

### Choose the property, then the evidence-gathering technique

| Validation check | Question in the room-swap case |
|---|---|
| Validity | Does the workflow preserve Ms. Lee's actual authority? |
| Consistency | Can FIFO, urgency and approval rules hold together? |
| Completeness | Are reason, cancellation and exceptions covered? |
| Realism | Can staff keep maintenance flags sufficiently current? |
| Verifiability | Can an objective test decide whether the timing rule holds? |

A **check** names the property; a **technique** gathers evidence. Reviews/inspections compare the written commitments; prototypes expose interaction gaps; test-case generation exposes missing expected outcomes; walkthroughs examine a scenario with stakeholders; model consistency checks compare diagrams/text; traceability analysis finds missing links. Combining techniques reduces different blind spots, without proving absence of every defect. [^validation]

**Visible worked test design:** SR-2.1 says reject if capacity is below enrollment. With capacity 30 and enrollment 45, 30 < 45 is true, so submission must be rejected with the specified capacity response. At capacity 45 the inequality is false: this rule alone does not reject, but other checks can still prevent approval. A test at equality therefore does not establish “always approve.” This is a derived test, not an executed result; the capacity-override variant below must be resolved for a final baseline.

## 6. What changes when a new rule arrives after sign-off?

![[10_UNIVERSITY_CS/Software Engineering 1/Unit 04 - Requirements Engineering and SRS/Attachments/requirement-lineage.svg]]

**Figure 2 — Requirement lineage (informal traceability diagram)**

**Read it:** Follow linked evidence, user need, detailed requirement, realization and test. Downward links indicate derivation/realization, not runtime sequence. Follow the relationships upward to recover the justification.

**Key takeaway:** A local edit can have nonlocal consequences. Existing standard figure reused unchanged; source principles: S06 §4.8.1 and ID glossary; S04 pp.65–67.

The **RTM** cross-references requirements, design/code, tests and status. Forward tracing finds missing realization; backward tracing challenges artifacts with no authorized purpose, including unrequested features (“gold plating”). Necessary infrastructure may have a derived architectural justification; missing linkage is a finding to investigate. [^trace]

**Worked impact sequence:** an ECR records request ID, submitter, description and reason. Locate affected requirements and versions in the RTM; follow modules, interfaces and tests; inspect shared dependencies; estimate implementation, data changes, retesting and milestone impact. The CCB weighs business, schedule/resource, technical and QA evidence. An approved request changes the baseline and dependent artifacts; rejection preserves the baseline and records why. [^change]

In the fictional example, the later ECR adds **NFR2**, affects SR-1.1 and TC-API-204, and produces v1.1. Code/API names in the reading are trace references, not executable files supplied here. “Passed” in the source's RTM is a story status, not test evidence from this production task. The displayed sign-off block remains blank v1.0, so it cannot prove actual v1.1 signatures.

## Revision 1.1 — From stakeholder wording to a verifiable specification

**Source comparison:** revision 1.1 moves EARS to §4.5.1 (PDF pp.32–37) and organizing requirements to §4.5.2 (pp.38–41). Their order in the earlier lecture is different; section labels alone cannot identify a page across versions.

On p.36, the E-003 sign-off concern becomes a requirement to keep a submitted room-swap request pending until the room administrator approves or rejects it. This clarifies the pending decision state. It still requires precise eligible transitions and exception rules before claiming every allocation path enforces approval. E-002's “allow submission” example is written without a WHEN trigger; a raw quote containing “When” does not automatically make the resulting specification event-driven.

### One need can generate several system requirements

PDF p.40 refines mobile schedule viewing into supported platforms (iOS 17+/Safari and Android 13+/Chrome), the displayed fields, matching mobile/desktop entries for the same student and term, and authentication before display. ### Problem O1 — Q7 — Approval before a quick swap

**ORIGINAL COURSE EXERCISE** · **Difficulty:** ★★☆

**Source:** Requirements Engineering - Reading Material.docx, End-of-Chapter Review Questions, Q7.

Rewrite the raw statement "the system should let students swap rooms quickly, but only if a lecturer approves it first" as one or more EARS-pattern requirements. Which pattern(s) did you use, and why?

> [!tip]- Hint
> Preserve this question’s student requester and lecturer approver: they differ from the main lecturer/admin case. Do not invent the meaning of quickly.

> [!success]- Solution
> **First clarify** whether swap means room-allocation change or only request submission, who the lecturer approver is, and which measurable deadline applies. Preserve the question’s roles. One proposed decomposition is: **WHEN a student submits a room-swap request, the system shall record it as pending lecturer approval** (event-driven); **IF lecturer approval has not been recorded, THEN the system shall prevent the requested allocation change** (unwanted behavior); **WHEN the authorized lecturer approves an eligible request, the system shall apply the approved room change** (event-driven). These separate request, authorization and effect. “Quickly” still requires a performance requirement with agreed timing endpoints, threshold and conditions; no number is given, so mark that target UNCONFIRMED and do not baseline it. These are proposed clarifications, not permission to replace the main case’s Ms. Lee sign-off rule.

### Problem E1 — Separate requirement levels and types

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material.docx`, §§4.5.2–4.6.3, and `Requirements Engineering - Lecture Slides.pdf`, PDF pp.35–46.

A student-facing request says: “Students need to see a readable room timetable on phones. The system shall return the timetable data for an authenticated student. At least 95% of complete timetable views shall render within 2 seconds under the agreed mobile workload.” Classify each statement by level and by functional/quality role, then explain why the labels are not interchangeable.

> [!tip]- Hint
> First ask who can read the statement and how much solution detail it contains; then ask whether it specifies behavior or a measurable quality constraint.

> [!success]- Solution
> “Students need to see a readable room timetable on phones” is a high-level user requirement: it expresses the stakeholder outcome without a complete technical contract. “The system shall return the timetable data for an authenticated student” is a more detailed system functional requirement because it specifies system behavior and an access condition. “At least 95% … within 2 seconds …” is a system non-functional performance requirement because it constrains measurable quality under stated conditions. User/system describes level and audience; functional/non-functional describes the kind of obligation. A detailed system requirement can be functional or non-functional, so one axis cannot replace the other.
>
> Source: course principles in the cited materials; scenario and thresholds are pedagogical.

### Problem I1 — Define a usable timing observation

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material.docx`, §4.6.3.

A proposed requirement says “show the result quickly.” For a synthetic room-search feature, propose a measurable target and identify the decisions needed before a real acceptance test.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Name the measured interval, threshold, population and operating conditions.

> [!success]- Solution
> One **proposed, non-official** target: with the agreed room dataset and query mix, under 50 concurrent users on the agreed network/device setup, at least 95% of searches shall render the complete result list within 2 seconds from search submission during a 10-minute observation window. Metric: end-to-end response time; threshold: ≥95% ≤2 seconds; conditions: stated workload/environment/window. Obtain agreement on dataset size, query mix, device/network conditions, failures/timeouts and sampling procedure. The values are illustrative choices, not SOE203 requirements or measured results.

### Problem D1 — Inspect an apparently passing test

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Requirements Engineering - Lecture Slides.pdf`, PDF pp.52,63; S11 p.24.

A test fetches schedule JSON after opening the schedule tab in 1.8 seconds. A reviewer marks login-to-calendar rendering within 3.0 seconds as passed. Explain the unsupported inference and design the missing evidence.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Compare trigger, measured endpoint and threshold before comparing the numbers.

> [!success]- Solution
> 1.8 seconds satisfies a <2.0-second JSON-fetch limit for that observation, assuming valid conditions. It does not measure login-to-complete-calendar rendering. The two source representations have different triggers, endpoints and thresholds. Resolve which version is authoritative; if both are explicitly agreed as separate constraints, test each interval under defined conditions and link each result to its requirement/version. Rendering or other delays could exceed the remaining budget. Do not invent a nested API budget or a final pass status from this one measurement.

### Problem I2 — Evaluate an interaction without erasing a control

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Requirements Engineering - Lecture Slides.pdf`, PDF p.47; S06 §4.5.4.

For a synthetic feature, A is timetable display, B is its 3-second performance budget, and C is a mandatory encryption control. Construct the course-style three-by-three grid and explain what evidence a negative pair requires.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Use symmetric pair judgments; distinguish potential resource competition from actual impossibility.

> [!success]- Solution
> Following the source’s illustrative relationships:
> 
> | Pair | A | B | C |
> |---|---:|---:|---:|
> | A | — | +1 | 0 |
> | B | +1 | — | −1 |
> | C | 0 | −1 | — |
> 
> A/B: responsiveness supports usable viewing. A/C: neutral in this narrow functional comparison. B/C: encryption may consume part of the timing budget. State these as justified qualitative judgments, not universal constants. Establish the data path and exact control, then measure or prototype representative timing. Seek a design satisfying both; if unavailable, present scope/resources/performance alternatives for authorization. Mandatory C cannot be silently discarded. A negative score alone is not evidence of a failed performance test.

### Problem T1 — Transfer traceability to changed approval authority

**SUPPLEMENTARY PRACTICE.** Synthetic scenario; principles from `Requirements Engineering - Reading Material.docx`, §§4.8.1–4.8.2, end-of-chapter Q27–Q28; transfer scenario is supplementary.

In a synthetic library reservation design, trained librarians initially approve reservations. A proposed change lets authenticated members reserve available copies directly. Explain what must be re-examined and produce one positive and one negative planned test. No implementation or institutional policy is supplied.

> [!tip]- Hint
> Trace the changed authority into behavior, permissions, concurrency, user interaction and tests.

> [!success]- Solution
> Record an ECR and the proposed policy; do not claim approval. Trace approval-related requirements into reservation creation, authorization, UI/pending states, notifications and tests, inspecting shared availability/concurrency rules. Determine whether librarian review disappears entirely or remains for exceptions; assess effort, risk and schedule before the authorized decision.
> 
> **Positive planned test, conditional on approval:** an authenticated eligible member requests an available copy; one reservation is committed for that member without waiting for librarian approval, and the copy becomes reserved.
> 
> **Negative planned test:** an unauthenticated requester attempts the same operation; no reservation is created and the specified authentication response is shown. The exact response still needs specification. Also retain concurrency checks so two members cannot both reserve the same copy. If approved, update the SRS baseline, trace links, design/test plans and change record; keep tests Planned until execution supplies results. Changing the approver does not remove independent availability and access constraints.
